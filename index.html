International Court of Justice · The Hague · 19 November 2026

Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, the International Court of Justice has ruled

By nine votes to six, the judges found that Myanmar’s security forces killed, raped and burned in northern Rakhine State in 2017 with the intent to destroy the Rohingya population there. This is a guide to what the court decided, where and when it happened, and what it has ordered. paras 467, 646

Before you read. This article describes mass killing, including the killing of children, and sexual violence. The most detailed testimony is kept in panels that stay closed unless you open them.

The ruling in brief

  • 9–6The vote that Myanmar committed genocide para 646
  • 10,000People killed, at the least. The court found this estimate supported by the evidence para 253
  • 700,000Rohingya, and more, who fled to Bangladesh in 2017 para 262
  • 392Villages, approximately, that were partly or wholly destroyed para 272
  1. What was decided. Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya of northern Rakhine State during military “clearance operations” in 2017. The court was “fully convinced” that its forces acted with intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya population of Myanmar. paras 468, 646
  2. What else the court found. Myanmar broke its duty to prevent genocide, by doing nothing to hold back civilians who joined the attacks alongside its forces (nine votes to six). It has failed to punish those responsible (11 to four). It has not passed the laws the Genocide Convention requires (14 to one). paras 488, 646
  3. What it did not find. By 14 votes to one, the court held that Myanmar had not directly and publicly incited private persons to commit genocide. It also found that one of the four kinds of genocidal act The Gambia alleged had not been conclusively proved. paras 372, 646
  4. What Myanmar must do. Investigate and, if warranted, prosecute and punish those suspected. Change its law. Allow displaced Rohingya to return safely, return or replace their property, and pay compensation where that is not enough. It has two years to report to the court on the restitution it has made and the compensation it proposes. paras 596, 615, 621, 625
  5. What Myanmar says. It denies genocide. It says its forces carried out counter-terrorism operations against an armed group, the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA), which had attacked police and army posts. The court accepted that the attacks happened, but found that many features of the operations were “inconsistent with an intent to carry out counter-insurgency operations”. paras 177, 444, 453

Numbers in small type, like para 253, give the paragraph of the judgment each statement comes from. The judgment is the only source for this article.

The court and the case

The International Court of Justice rules on disputes between states. It does not put people on trial. paras 295, 579

The case was brought under the Genocide Convention, a treaty adopted by the United Nations general assembly on 9 December 1948. The convention lets one state take another to the court over how the treaty has been applied. paras 1, 579

That is why this judgment names a country, not a person. The court stressed that a state’s responsibility under the convention is a matter of international law and is “not of a criminal nature”. Whether any individual is guilty of a crime is a separate question. para 295

The Gambia filed the case on 11 November 2019. It did not have to show that it had been harmed itself. In 2022 the court ruled that any state that is party to the convention may bring a case against another to make it comply, because all of them share an interest in the treaty being kept. paras 1, 17, 585, 586

Eleven more states joined the case to argue about how the convention should be read: Belgium, Canada, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Denmark, France, Germany, Ireland, the Maldives, the Netherlands, Slovenia and the United Kingdom. paras 32, 37

  1. 11 November 2019The Gambia files its case. para 1
  2. 23 January 2020The court orders Myanmar, while the case is heard, to prevent genocidal acts against the Rohingya and to preserve evidence. para 8
  3. 1 February 2021The military seizes power in Myanmar. para 77
  4. 22 July 2022The court rejects Myanmar’s objections and rules that it can hear the case. paras 16, 17
  5. 12 to 29 January 2026Public hearings. Three Rohingya witnesses and one witness for Myanmar give evidence in closed session. paras 58, 60
  6. 19 November 2026Judgment. para 646

To find genocide the court requires evidence that is “fully conclusive”. It must be “fully convinced” that the allegations “have been clearly established”. para 80

Where this happened

Myanmar, known as Burma until 1989, is in mainland south-east Asia. China lies to the north and north-east, Laos and Thailand to the east, and India and Bangladesh to the west. Myanmar puts its population at more than 54 million. paras 64, 66

Rakhine State

Rakhine State is a strip of coast in the far west of the country, facing the Bay of Bengal. About 3.2 million people lived there at the 2014 census. Most are ethnic Rakhine, a mainly Buddhist people. paras 65, 67

The region was once known as Arakan. A mountain range separates it from central Myanmar. From the fifteenth to the eighteenth century much of it belonged to an independent kingdom, Mrauk U. para 70

Three townships

The events in this case took place in the north of the state, in three townships: Maungdaw, Buthidaung and Rathedaung. Two of them border Bangladesh. paras 69, 272

Where the Rohingya lived

Before the 2017 operations about 815,000 Rohingya lived in these three townships. paras 69, 272

Who the Rohingya are

A mainly Muslim people of Rakhine State, whom Myanmar does not recognise by that name.

The Rohingya speak a language of their own, also called Rohingya. It is mostly spoken, not written, and is closely related to the Chittagonian language of neighbouring Bangladesh. para 68

Myanmar’s government calls them “Bengali”. It officially recognises 135 “national ethnic groups”. The Rohingya are not on the list. paras 66, 67, 68

Myanmar and The Gambia disagreed about when the ancestors of the Rohingya arrived. The court found that the Rohingya have lived in what is now Myanmar “for generations”. It cited a record from 1799 of Muslims who have long [been] settled in Arakan, and who call themselves Rooinga. Myanmar itself accepted that Bengali-speakers were living in Arakan before British rule. paras 134, 135

This matters in law. The Genocide Convention protects national, ethnic, racial and religious groups. The court found that the Rohingya of Myanmar are a distinct ethnic group, with their own language and culture, and so are protected. During the hearings Myanmar conceded that they are at least a “part” of a protected group. It maintained that they belong to the same group as the people living just across the border in Bangladesh. paras 308, 309, 310

How it came to this

The court traced decades of laws and military operations that left the Rohingya stateless, restricted and exposed.

The court did not treat the operations as a sudden event. It set out a long record of what it called discrimination against the Rohingya, rendering the Rohingya marginalized and particularly vulnerable. para 400

  1. 1784

    Arakan is conquered

    Burma’s Konbaung dynasty conquers Arakan and makes it part of the Burmese empire. para 70

  2. 1826

    British rule begins

    After the first Anglo-Burmese war, Arakan passes to the British East India Company. In the colonial period a large number of Muslims move to the area from what is now Bangladesh. paras 71, 135

  3. 1942

    Wartime violence

    Fighting between Buddhists and Muslims at the village of Alethankyaw leaves thousands dead on both sides. para 72

  4. 1948

    Independence

    Burma becomes independent on 4 January. In the years that follow, the court found, the Rohingya were treated as part of the country. Myanmar accepts that tens of thousands of them received citizenship. paras 71, 136

  5. 1962

    The army takes power

    The armed forces, known as the Tatmadaw, remove the civilian government in a coup. They rule for more than five decades. para 73

  6. 1978

    An exodus to Bangladesh

    A military “registration operation”, accompanied by violence, drives an estimated 200,000 Rohingya into Bangladesh. Nearly all are later sent back under an agreement between Myanmar and Bangladesh. para 73

  7. 1982

    The citizenship law

    A new law ties full citizenship to “national races” and lets the state decide which groups count. The law does not name the Rohingya, but the list the state draws up leaves them out. They were, the court found, formally excluded for the first time from those entitled to citizenship based on race or ethnic origin. para 137

  8. 1991–1992

    A second operation

    A similar operation displaces an estimated 200,000 Rohingya to Bangladesh. The great majority are later repatriated. para 73

  9. 1993

    Marriage rules

    The border security force in Maungdaw notes that the population is rising fast and that most residents are Muslims. It orders that “Islamic” marriages need special permission, for the good of the region and the race. para 143

  10. 2005

    Limits on children

    A regional order in Maungdaw says that people given permission to marry must limit the number of children. It stays in force until 2018. On paper the limit was not confined to Muslims, the court noted, but officials described the rules as Processes for Controlling Bengali Population. Penalties were not always enforced. One witness told the court she had to sign a paper promising to have no more than two children, and hid one child from inspectors. paras 143, 144, 145

  11. 2012

    Violence and camps

    Fighting breaks out between Buddhist and Muslim communities in Rakhine State, with the security forces also involved. More than 100,000 displaced Rohingya are confined in camps. Myanmar told the court that, as of November 2025, more than 100,000 were still there. paras 74, 151

  12. 2013

    An armed group forms

    Rohingya insurgents form a group first called Harakah al-Yaqin and later the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army, or ARSA. para 75

  13. 2016

    A civilian government

    A civilian government takes office in March, after a general election in November 2015. para 73

  14. 9 October 2016

    ARSA attacks police headquarters

    ARSA attacks three Border Guard Police headquarters. Nine police officers and eight attackers are killed. The army’s first “clearance operation” follows, and about 87,000 Rohingya flee to Bangladesh. para 175

  15. Summer 2017

    Aid agencies are shut out

    A World Food Programme report in July finds nearly one third of people in northern Rakhine severely food insecure. The government then bans the agency from the area. Médecins Sans Frontières is made to stop work there two weeks before the operations begin. para 150

  16. 10 August 2017

    Troops are flown in

    More than 1,600 soldiers of the 33rd and 99th Light Infantry Divisions are airlifted into Rakhine State. Myanmar says this answered ARSA’s growing activity. In the months before, the authorities had also confiscated anything that could be used as a weapon from Rohingya and ordered fences taken down. Myanmar calls these counter-terrorism measures. The court said they foreseeably left Rohingya civilians less able to protect themselves. paras 179, 180

  17. 24 August 2017

    The Annan commission reports

    A commission set up by Myanmar and chaired by the former UN secretary-general Kofi Annan delivers its final report. It finds that only about 4,000 Muslims in Rakhine State are recognised as citizens, leaving approximately one million stateless. para 140

The language used about them

After 2012, the court found, talk that disparaged and dehumanized the Rohingya spread through the media and the sermons of some monks. Myanmar accepted that hate speech among the public was a concern. It denied that the government or the army ran any campaign. paras 154, 155

The court found otherwise. Facebook was Myanmar’s most widely used social network. In August 2018 the company removed 43 pages, among them pages in the name of the army’s commander-in-chief and his office. UN investigators later found 10,485 pieces of anti-Rohingya hate speech on 36 of those pages from July to December 2017 alone, and evidence of a coordinated network of pages with clear ties to the Myanmar military. paras 155, 161, 162, 163

Myanmar said that any soldiers or police who ran such pages were acting outside the scope of their employment. The court noted that it offered no evidence against the investigators’ findings. para 163

On 1 September 2017, a week into the operations, the commander-in-chief, Senior General Min Aung Hlaing, spoke at a fundraiser for the forces taking part and for Rakhine civilians who had fled the ARSA attacks. The Bengali problem was a long-standing one which has become an unfinished job despite the efforts of the previous governments to solve it, he said, and: absolutely, our country has no Rohingya race. Myanmar told the court such passages had been taken out of context. The court said their meaning was not substantially changed when read in full. It found that his speeches, as reported, were capable of fuelling hatred against the Rohingya. paras 157, 159, 402

What the posts said Contains dehumanising language and slurs

The UN’s investigative mechanism found that posts on the military network played on ideas such as these: that the Rohingya pose an existential threat to Myanmar through violence, terrorism or ‘Islamisation’, and that they pose a threat to Burmese racial purity through their alleged rampant breeding. para 164

A video posted on at least four pages of the network on 28 August 2017 shows a uniformed soldier speaking to a group of people. He calls the Rohingya “animals” and says the army will clear the villages where those animals live. He tells his listeners: [I]f you can carry a sword, then carry a sword, if you can carry a stick, then carry a stick. para 165

The day before the operations began, the commander-in-chief’s office posted that troops had repelled an attack by 200 “Bengali”. Of the 685 comments posted in reply, more than 200 explicitly called for the Rohingya villagers, as well as Rohingya and/or Muslims in general, to be shot, killed, exterminated or permanently removed from Myanmar. para 168

How the court established the facts

Myanmar kept UN investigators out. The court had to weigh the evidence itself.

In its earlier genocide judgments, on Bosnia and Croatia, the court could rely on the verdicts of the international criminal tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, which had access to the crime scenes. Here there were no such verdicts. para 83

Myanmar did not allow the UN’s fact-finding mission, or the investigative body that followed it, to enter the country, and did not answer their requests for information. The second body sent 26 such requests. Myanmar said it distrusted the UN human rights council that set them up and was under no duty to let them in. paras 76, 128, 129

The court agreed that a state is not, as a rule, obliged to let UN bodies in. But because Myanmar had kept investigators out and had not genuinely investigated the events itself, the court said it could draw inferences more freely about events that only Myanmar could fully know and had not satisfactorily explained. paras 130, 131

What the evidence was, and how much weight it got
  • Witnesses in court. Four people who lived through the violence of late August 2017 gave evidence in closed session. Three were Rohingya called by The Gambia, from the villages of Min Gyi, Chut Pyin and Maung Nu. The court found them credible. One was called by Myanmar and described an attack by ARSA on Hindu villagers. The court found her account consistent with the record. paras 87, 88
  • The UN fact-finding mission. It carried out 1,294 interviews in all. It used a lower standard of proof than the court does, so the court said it had been cautious. Even so it found the mission’s two main reports to be generally reliable. para 109
  • The UN’s investigative mechanism for Myanmar. Its signed witness statements, used by both sides, were given considerable evidential weight. para 95
  • Satellite images and fire data. Myanmar did not question that the images were authentic. The court gave them considerable evidential weight. para 121
  • Myanmar’s own records. The court treated parts of them with caution. The army’s reports do not say how many civilians were killed or hurt, and do not explain the large number of rounds fired. para 125
  • Reports by charities and campaign groups. Both sides used them. The court gave them less weight than the rest. para 124

Myanmar argued that the fact-finding mission’s reports deserved no evidential weight: it had not taken part, the mission was biased against it, and the reports rested on interviews and so were hearsay. para 108

August 2017

25 August

ARSA attacks at least 30 police and army outposts across northern Rakhine. The group is estimated to have 40 to 70 firearms; most of its members carry blades and sticks. paras 173, 177

Myanmar says 13 members of its security forces were killed in the 2017 attacks and the operations that followed. para 174

25 August and the day after

ARSA also killed civilians

The court found credible evidence of this. In two villages in the Kha Maung Seik area up to 99 Hindus were killed on 25 August. Another attack the next day killed six more. In the days after, ARSA’s commander ordered non-Rohingya villages to be burned, and the court was satisfied that the group burned two. paras 177, 178

25 August

The security forces respond within hours

Security forces are sent into Rohingya villages near the outposts that were attacked, among them Ah Lel Than Kyaw and Myin Hlut. Myanmar calls these “clearance operations”, its term for counter-insurgency. paras 69, 181

27 August

The operations widen

Several units now work at once across all three townships. Chut Pyin and Maung Nu are attacked on the same day. para 181

28 August

Separate units move on Gu Dar Pyin and Koe Tan Kauk. Some people who flee one village shelter in another that is attacked days later. paras 181, 182, 227, 235

30 August

Min Gyi

Soldiers enter a village with a river on three sides. The court said it could not fix the exact number of dead. A count compiled by Rohingya refugees, which it found supported by the evidence, puts it at 750. paras 186, 199

1 September

Inn Din

Soldiers take ten men from a crowd gathered on the beach. They are killed the next day. Myanmar later acknowledged that its army was involved. para 232

5 September

Myanmar says the operations end

Myanmar gives this as the official end, while accepting that there were scattered incidents later. The court found that the burning and destruction of villages went on after this date. para 184

The same pattern, village after village

The UN fact-finding mission verified operations in 54 places and had first-hand accounts from 22 more. The court saw a degree of consistency across a wide geographic area: in timing, in weapons, in what was done. para 248

It found deliberate executions, indiscriminate shooting, the shooting of people as they ran, burnings, and the killing of children and infants. The UN mission found that in at least 22 of the places it studied, nearby Rakhine villages were generally left intact. paras 251, 411

Nine places the court examined

The court looked closely at three villages, one in each township, and at six more that UN investigators had studied in detail. paras 185, 225

Each entry gives what the court found, the numbers it accepted or noted, and what Myanmar said, where the judgment records it. Names are the Burmese ones the court used, with the Rohingya name in brackets. Positions on the map are approximate. para 185

Min Gyi (Tula Toli)

Maungdaw township · 30 August 2017 · 99th Light Infantry Division para 186

A mainly Rohingya village with a river on three sides. Soldiers came in from the north on the morning of 30 August, with ethnic Rakhine and members of other ethnic groups from nearby villages, who carried long knives and machetes. They opened fire and began burning houses. paras 186, 188

People who ran to the riverbank were cornered there. Soldiers shot into the crowd. Some who tried to swim across were shot or drowned. Those left on the bank were rounded up, the men separated from the women and children and shot. Women and girls were taken into houses and raped, and the houses were then set on fire. paras 190, 191, 192, 195, 197

Satellite images show the Rohingya village completely destroyed. The Rakhine settlement beside it was intact. para 198

Deaths
750, by a count the refugee community compiled, including at least 400 villagers and others who had fled there. The court said it could not determine the exact number but found these figures supported by the evidence. para 199
Destroyed
About 440 houses and other buildings. para 198
Myanmar’s account
The operation answered ARSA violence nearby on 25 and 26 August. Myanmar did not say any ARSA operation took place inside the village first, though the court noted some ARSA activity across the river and signs of skirmishes there between 26 and 29 August. Myanmar said it killed 101 ARSA fighters here. No guns were seized. paras 173, 187
Testimony the court relied on Describes the killing of children

A survivor, known to the court as Witness MS, gave evidence in person. He described soldiers trapping villagers against the shore: then they targeted and intentionally started shooting towards the people who gathered in that area. para 190

The children were running in the shore and they were crying, the children who were close to the military, the military took them and threw them in the water or on the fire.

Witness MS. Six members of his family were killed that day, five of them children. para 194

The court found that children were deliberately shot, killed with knives or thrown into the river or onto fires. para 194

Chut Pyin (Shuap Praung)

Rathedaung township · 27 August 2017 · 33rd Light Infantry Division para 200

Soldiers surrounded the village at about 2pm on 27 August and opened fire. With them were armed Rakhine men, some in military-style uniforms. para 203

Men and boys were separated from women and younger children. Witnesses described men being led away from a paddy field, then gunfire. Women were raped, some at a school the army was using as a base. People were shot as they ran across the rice fields. paras 205, 206, 209

Satellite images show Chut Pyin entirely destroyed. The Rakhine village next to it was left intact. para 211

Deaths
358, including 127 children aged five or under, on a list drawn up by refugees. The court said it could not confirm the figures but found them credible. para 212
Myanmar’s account
The village was a particularly important foothold for ARSA. Troops went in after one person threw a bomb at the police outpost on 27 August and ran towards the village, where they met several hundred attackers. The court noted that the statements backing this were inconsistent and came from people in Myanmar’s custody. The army recorded firing 1,700 rounds, killing 32 people it called terrorists and recovering two home-made guns, neither of which worked. paras 173, 201, 202, 212
The court’s view
It could not rule out that ARSA members were present. But the evidence, it said, indicates that the operation was primarily directed against civilians. para 202
Testimony the court relied on Describes rape and the killing of a child

Witness NJ gave evidence in person. She told the court that soldiers beat her husband and father-in-law and took them away. She never saw them again. para 205

Her child was taken from her arms, stabbed and thrown into a burning house. She was then beaten, stabbed and raped by two soldiers in her home until she lost consciousness. paras 206, 208

Another witness said the gunfire came from every side, and that so many bullets looked like drops of rain. para 204

Maung Nu (Monu Para)

Buthidaung township · 27 August 2017 · 564th Light Infantry Regiment para 213

The village was sheltering people who had fled an ARSA attack, and the security forces’ operation that followed it, in a neighbouring village on 25 August. Soldiers arrived at about 9am and started firing. Many villagers ran to the compound of a man they believed was on good terms with the security forces. paras 182, 217, 218

Soldiers forced them out. Men and older boys were separated from the women, their hands tied behind them, made to lie face down and beaten. They were then taken away in groups of five or six and killed. paras 219, 220

The court noted that here women and girls do not appear to have been targeted for execution, though it accepted accounts that some were raped. para 221

Deaths
At least 82 named on a list by the refugee community. The UN mission estimated that up to 100 people were executed. The court said the number is difficult to estimate but found these figures supported by the evidence. para 224
Destroyed
More than 320 buildings burned between Maung Nu and the next village. para 223
Myanmar’s account
Myanmar gave two explanations, which the court called difficult to reconcile. One was a raid on an ARSA cell leader, though the house attacked belonged to another man with the same name. The other was a reply to an attack by about 100 militants, for which it offered only its own internal report, with nothing to corroborate it. para 216
ARSA here
The court found some ARSA presence in the village before the operation. A witness both sides relied on said the group had patrolled the streets, killed suspected informants and tried to make crude bombs, and that an ARSA leader lived there. He put its numbers at between 20 and 60, with rudimentary weapons. Three ARSA attacks were reported close by on 25 August. paras 214, 215
Testimony the court relied on Describes executions

Witness MN gave evidence in person. He said he heard an officer make a phone call asking what to do with the 87 people who had been caught. para 220

He was later made to walk, hands tied behind his back, to a place where he saw piles of bodies. His father’s was among them. His eight-year-old nephew was also killed that day. para 220

Another witness said soldiers took away her two sons, aged 9 and 12, and made them lie face down with the adults. She never saw [her] two sons again. para 220

Gu Dar Pyin (Gudam Para)

Buthidaung township · 28 and 29 August 2017 paras 226, 227

Security forces surrounded the village and fired on people trying to leave, children among them. Houses were burned. Ethnic Rakhine people who came with the soldiers captured Rohingya and killed or wounded them, and helped burn houses. para 227

Bodies were put into pits. Villagers who went back some days later described seeing them in shallow graves. Satellite images confirm that the whole Rohingya village was burned. paras 228, 229

Deaths
243, by the estimate of Rohingya community volunteers. para 229
Myanmar’s account
Official reports said the army was answering an attack by 500 ARSA terrorists on 28 August. No other evidence before the court supported this. para 226
Afterwards
After a news agency reported the mass graves, two officers were sentenced to one year in prison. para 544
Testimony the court relied on Describes the death of children

One woman told UN investigators she had to choose to try to save her two younger children when her older girl and boy were attacked. The next day her husband dug through pits of bodies until he found their son. They never found their daughter. para 229

Inn Din (Aung Dung)

Maungdaw township · about a week from 25 August 2017 paras 230, 231

Soldiers, with Rakhine villagers alongside them, moved through the Rohingya settlements here for about a week. They came shooting, often in the early morning, and burned houses, at times with people still inside. para 231

On 1 September, as hundreds of Rohingya were gathered on the beach, soldiers picked out ten men. They were marched off, tied, and made to sit in a row. The next day they were killed: one was beheaded, one had his throat cut and eight were shot. para 232

How it became known
Through reporting by Reuters journalists. Myanmar then acknowledged that its army had been involved, in breach of its own rules of engagement. para 232
Myanmar’s account
The operations followed ARSA attacks on police posts in neighbouring villages on 25 August, which the court accepted took place. Myanmar also said ARSA attacked Inn Din on 29 August. Nothing else before the court supported that. para 230
Afterwards
Four officers and three soldiers were sentenced to ten years in prison. All were pardoned by the commander-in-chief after eight months. Myanmar’s first reaction to the report was to arrest and prosecute the journalists, who were jailed for more than twice as long as the soldiers. paras 544, 548
Destroyed
Most, if not all, Rohingya buildings. The Rakhine village nearby was unaffected. para 233

Koe Tan Kauk and Chein Khar Li (Ko Tan Kaung and Ku Lar)

Rathedaung township · 25 and 28 August 2017 paras 234, 235

Two neighbouring villages on the coast. At Chein Khar Li on 25 August, security forces fired on villagers as they tried to get away and burned the village from south to north. At Koe Tan Kauk on 28 August, weapons were also fired from two navy ships that had been offshore since the middle of the month. para 235

Some people who could not run, among them the old, children and people with disabilities, were burned inside their houses. para 236

Deaths
More than 180 in the two villages, according to information gathered by the refugee community. para 238
Where the court held back
The UN mission reported rapes in both villages. The court took note but said the evidence was not enough, because nothing else confirmed it. para 237
Myanmar’s account
The operations answered repeated ARSA attacks involving hundreds of armed fighters. Nothing else before the court supported this. para 234

Kyauk Pan Du (Shitar Fawrikka)

Maungdaw township · late August 2017, at least two days paras 240, 241

ARSA attacked a Border Guard Police camp here on 25 August. In the army operation that followed, soldiers fired automatic weapons at people as they ran and used knives on those who were caught or wounded. paras 240, 241

Nearly all Rohingya property in the village was burned. Some people who tried to get out of burning houses were pushed back in. The court found credible evidence that women and girls were raped. para 241

Myanmar’s account
Reinforcements sent on 28 August were met by 30-40 terrorists. Nothing else before the court supported this. para 240

Ah Lel Than Kyaw (Hassu Rata)

Maungdaw township · three days from 25 August 2017 paras 242, 243

ARSA attacked two Border Guard Police posts here on 25 August. Myanmar says the attack involved hundreds of armed men, lasted between one and three hours, and left one official and 17 attackers dead. A police official said he had been warned in advance and had moved Rakhine civilians to safety. para 242

A three-day operation by the army and police followed. The court found continuous, indiscriminate shooting at Rohingya civilians, young children and old people among them. Most Rohingya property was destroyed. Witnesses later saw up to 15 bodies in a well in the market. para 243

Deaths
Up to 77 on a list by Rohingya villagers. The UN mission could not verify the figure but found it consistent with its interviews. para 244
The Gambia’s point
It did not dispute that ARSA was active here. It said any threat had been dealt with before the operation began. para 242

Myin Hlut (May Rulla)

Maungdaw township · several days from 25 August 2017 paras 245, 246

ARSA attacked a police outpost here on 25 August. The army operation began the same morning. One witness described about 300 soldiers and police surrounding the village and shooting as people fled. He was shot in the leg, an injury confirmed by medical records made at the time. paras 245, 246

The UN mission reported that soldiers also killed people with knives and that some women and girls were raped. para 246

Deaths
Up to 70 on a list by the refugee community. The UN mission could not verify it but found it consistent with what it had heard. para 247
Afterwards
UN investigators documented the building of a Border Guard Police base in the village as of 2021. para 641

The scale

Each dot stands for ten people. Together they are the 815,000 or so Rohingya who lived in northern Rakhine before the operations. para 69

The dots are scattered at random inside each township. They show numbers, not where anyone lived.

More than 700,000 fled

They crossed into Bangladesh. That is over 85% of the Rohingya who were still in northern Rakhine after the violence of 2016. para 262

Some walked for two weeks through jungle and mountains and across rivers swollen by the monsoon, with no shelter and little food or water. para 262

At least 10,000 killed, on the estimate the court accepted

The figure comes from the UN fact-finding mission, which called it conservative. The court found it supported by the record. A separate survey of more than 11,000 refugees by Médecins Sans Frontières put violent deaths in the first month at no fewer than 6,700 to 9,867. paras 250, 252, 253

Myanmar’s own count for 2017 was 13 members of its security forces, 376 ARSA fighters and 131 other people. It accepted that it had not estimated deaths among civilians who took no part in the fighting. para 249

Children

The court cited estimates that at least 730, and as many as 2,109, Rohingya children under five died from violence in that month. It found the killing of children, including the very young, was widespread and intentional. paras 259, 422

Rape

Lists compiled by Rohingya volunteers record at least 1,834 people raped. The court found the figure supported by the evidence. It said the true number is hard to know, partly because of the stigma of reporting. para 258

It found that gang rape and mass rape were a recurring feature of the violence. para 254

The journey

Not everyone was attacked on the way. The court recorded credible accounts from people whom soldiers let pass. para 269

But it was convinced that the security forces systematically shot at Rohingya as they were fleeing their villages, and that a significant number were killed, raped or attacked on the road to Bangladesh. Myanmar had laid landmines on some roads and border crossings. paras 267, 270, 460

Nine years on

For more than nine years, the court said, most of those who survived the journey have lived in squalid and at times dangerous refugee camps, with no chance to go back and no homes to go back to. para 332

The villages

There were 993 villages in the three townships. Each block here is one of them. para 272

This is a count, not a map. Which blocks change is arbitrary. Only the numbers are real.

About 392 were burned or broken

Satellite images taken up to March 2018 show that, after 25 August 2017, 178 villages were totally destroyed and 214 partly destroyed. That is about 40% of all the villages in the three townships. para 272

About 326,000 people had lived in them. para 272

Who did it

Myanmar did not dispute that the burning took place. It suggested ARSA or unknown people might be responsible. The court found that ARSA burned two villages, and that the evidence did not support the idea of systematic burning by anyone other than the state. It was primarily Myanmar’s security forces, the court concluded, that burned the Rohingya villages. paras 178, 275, 276

Then the bulldozers

The UN fact-finding mission found that, as of December 2017, the government had begun clearing the burned sites with heavy machinery. In 2018 UN satellite analysts estimated that 78 of the destroyed villages showed signs of bulldozing or clearance, in most cases across the whole village. para 277

In all, they estimate, about 40,600 buildings were destroyed across Maungdaw, Buthidaung and Rathedaung. para 277

What was built in their place

New buildings went up quickly on the cleared land. At least seven Rohingya villages have been replaced by Border Guard Police bases. Houses were handed to families from other ethnic groups. paras 278, 280, 551

The court called it a deliberate, organized process of destroying Rohingya villages and farmland. para 282

Why the court called it genocide

In law, genocide is not decided by a count of the dead. It turns on what was done, and on why. paras 288, 317

The convention defines genocide as any of five kinds of act committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such. Both halves must be proved: the acts, and the intent. It is the intent that sets genocide apart from other grave crimes. paras 285, 286, 288

First, the acts

The Gambia alleged four of the five. The court found three of them proved. paras 311, 372

ProvedKilling members of the group

The court was fully convinced that the security forces intentionally killed Rohingya on a large scale. The killings included the direct targeting of civilians, women and very young children among them, and widespread indiscriminate shooting. paras 318, 319

Myanmar argued that The Gambia had not proved those killed were anything other than ARSA fighters or people caught in the crossfire. para 314

ProvedCausing serious bodily or mental harm

Bodily harm from shooting, knives, burning and rape. And mental harm: to people who escaped death or saw relatives killed, to those who still do not know what became of their families, and to those driven out with no home to return to. paras 329, 330, 332

The court said that being forced to flee is not, in itself, an act of genocide. It was the circumstances of this flight, taken together, that caused serious mental harm. para 333

Not provedInflicting conditions of life calculated to destroy the group physically

The Gambia argued that decades of limits on movement, food and healthcare, together with the violence, destruction and flight of 2017, met this test. The court agreed the restrictions were real, and that Rohingya children faced serious malnutrition in 2017. But it was not convinced that they were capable of causing the death of a substantial part of the group. And the 2017 operations, which were extreme violence over a short time, were not conditions of life of the kind this clause covers. paras 340, 341, 353, 355, 356

ProvedImposing measures intended to prevent births

The court pointed to the limits on marriage and children in Maungdaw, where nearly 500,000 Rohingya lived; the refusal to register Rohingya births; and the rapes of 2017, aimed mainly at women and girls of childbearing age. It set these against years of official warnings about Rohingya population growth. paras 366, 370, 371

Myanmar said the birth limits applied in one township only, did not apply only to Muslims, and that there was no evidence they were strictly enforced. para 359

Not allegedForcibly transferring children to another group

The Gambia did not claim this. para 311

Second, the intent

There was no direct evidence of a plan to destroy the Rohingya. The court therefore had to judge intent from what was done. Its rule for doing so is strict: genocidal intent must be the only reasonable inference from the facts. paras 382, 384, 389

It first found that the group targeted was the 815,000 or so Rohingya of northern Rakhine State, a substantial part of the Rohingya of Myanmar. paras 379, 380

Four things, taken together, pointed to an intent to destroy them: para 397

  • The background. Decades of discriminatory law, a hate campaign backed by the army, earlier military operations and mass displacement. paras 399, 436
  • Who was chosen. Rohingya villages were attacked and burned; Rakhine villages beside them were left alone. paras 411, 415
  • What was attacked. Sexual violence that degraded victims and their communities to an extreme degree, and the targeting of children. It is difficult to explain the targeting of children on any basis other than their membership in the protected group, the court said. paras 420, 422
  • How it was done. Broadly the same methods, by several units, in many places, and on a large scale. paras 181, 426

Third, the other explanations

If the facts could reasonably be explained in another way, the claim of genocide would fail. Myanmar pointed to others. The court rejected each of them. paras 438, 467

RejectedIt was counter-insurgency

This was Myanmar’s chief alternative: its forces were answering violent attacks by ARSA and its supporters, in the villages where ARSA was present. paras 444, 445

The court accepted part of this. Every place it examined was near an ARSA attack, real or claimed. But it found no evidence that troops told fighters from civilians. The army’s own reports did not. And the execution of men and boys, mass rape, the targeting of children and babies, the methodical burning of villages and attacks on people in flight are not consistent with an intent to conduct counter-insurgency operations. paras 452, 453

RejectedIt was meant to drive them out

Myanmar denied any aim of “ethnic cleansing”. But it argued that the court could reasonably read the evidence that way, and if so genocide would not be the only explanation. In its earlier cases on Bosnia and Croatia, the court had at times treated an aim of expelling people as pointing away from genocide. paras 447, 457

Here, unlike in those cases, the court found no evidence of any organised effort or plan by the government to transfer or evacuate the Rohingya. Soldiers attacked and killed people as they left, and on the road. Mines were laid at some border crossings. Some people were ordered to stay, or to go back to villages that had been burned. An aim of forcing people out, the court said, did not explain all of this. paras 458, 459, 460, 462

RejectedIt was punishment

Myanmar suggested the court could infer an aim of punishing the civilian population. The court took this to mean punishment for what ARSA had done. paras 449, 463

It answered that what was done went well beyond what would be required to punish the population, and that the destruction of a group is itself an extreme form of punishment. paras 464, 466

Myanmar’s argument about numbers

Myanmar argued that even 10,000 deaths would be 0.7% of the 1.4 million or so Rohingya in the country at the time: too small a share to show an intent to destroy them. para 428

The court answered that the right comparison is with the 815,000 people targeted, which makes it 1.2%; that a lower share of deaths does not, by itself, rule out genocidal intent; and that killing is not the only act the convention forbids, so the victims of the other acts count too. paras 429, 430

Myanmar also said its forces had chances to destroy the Rohingya and did not take them. Hundreds of thousands still live in Rakhine State, and some 120,000 already living in camps for displaced people inside Myanmar were not harmed. The court replied that a state need not take every chance to do harm for the intent to be there. paras 431, 434, 435

The only reasonable inference that can explain Myanmar’s actions is an intention to destroy a substantial part of the protected group.

The court para 467

What the court did not find

The Gambia did not win every point.

  • Incitement of private persons. The court found that some posts on the military’s Facebook network tried to encourage non-Rohingya people to use violence against the Rohingya. It was not persuaded that the evidence conclusively showed they were made with intent to destroy the group, were a direct appeal to commit genocide, or would have been taken as one. The commander-in-chief’s speeches, it said, appear to have been aimed mainly at his own forces. Any incitement of the state’s own forces is absorbed by the finding of genocide itself. paras 490, 502, 505, 506
  • Living conditions as a genocidal act. As set out above, the court did not accept that the long-standing restrictions, or the violence of 2017, met this part of the convention’s test. paras 355, 356
  • The civilians who joined in. Non-Rohingya civilians entered villages with the security forces and took part in killing and burning. The court could not say whether they themselves acted with genocidal intent. It held that Myanmar had the means to restrain them and manifestly chose not to use them. paras 482, 486, 488
  • Killings since 2020. The Gambia said Rohingya had been killed in breach of the court’s order of January 2020. The court said the evidence was too thin to show this. It found Myanmar had breached only the part of the order about preserving evidence. paras 633, 637, 642
  • Citizenship for all. The Gambia asked the court to order Myanmar to give full citizenship to every Rohingya. The court said that went beyond what was appropriate as a guarantee against repetition in this case. It did order protection from discrimination, including in access to citizenship. paras 603, 616

How the judges voted

Fifteen judges sat. They voted on 13 questions. Only the last was unanimous. para 646

Neither country had a judge of its own nationality on the bench, so each chose one to sit for this case: Navanethem Pillay, chosen by The Gambia, and Claus Kress, chosen by Myanmar. paras 7, 646

    What the court ordered, and what happens next

    Myanmar has two years to tell the court what it has given back, and what it will pay where it cannot. para 625

    Myanmar must

    • Investigate. Take immediate and effective steps to investigate everyone reasonably suspected of genocide or the related offences in the convention in connection with the 2017 operations and, if warranted, prosecute and punish them. The court said this duty reaches commanders, not only the soldiers under them. paras 522, 596
    • Change the law. Genocide was not a crime in Myanmar’s law until 2021. The court found the 2021 law still falls short. paras 562, 572, 573, 574
    • Let people return. Allow and help the safe, voluntary and dignified return of displaced Rohingya to where they lived, and the reunion of families. para 615
    • Give back what was taken. Return, or replace in kind, the property Rohingya lost or had damaged. para 615
    • Find the dead. Help search for the disappeared, and recover, identify and rebury the bodies of those killed, in keeping with Rohingya traditions. para 615
    • Care for survivors. Provide for the physical and psychological rehabilitation of the injured. para 615
    • Protect the Rohingya. From discrimination and persecution, including in access to citizenship and freedom of movement. paras 615, 616
    • Pay compensation. Where restitution is impossible, compensate the Rohingya, as individuals and as a group. The court will settle the amount in a later phase of the case. paras 621, 625
    • Guarantee it will not happen again. The court suggested legal reform, training for the security forces and effective civilian control of them, but left the choice of further means to Myanmar. para 604

    Where things stand

    The court found that Myanmar has not charged, prosecuted or punished anyone, soldier or civilian, for genocide or the related offences in the convention. There has been no systematic investigation of crimes by the military in 2017. Courts martial dealt with events in three places. None of those convicted served more than a year. paras 548, 549

    Evidence has been lost. The court found that Myanmar burned or otherwise disposed of bodies, bulldozed the sites and built on them. It also found that Myanmar broke the court’s 2020 order to preserve evidence: the remains of at least five Rohingya villages were burned in May 2020, and a police base was finished in 2021 on land where villages had stood. paras 552, 642

    Myanmar continues to deny that the harms the court described ever occurred. para 602

    The man who commanded Myanmar’s armed forces during the operations, Min Aung Hlaing, became acting head of the military government when the army seized power on 1 February 2021. After elections in December 2025 and January 2026 he was declared president in April 2026. In 2018 the UN fact-finding mission found enough information to warrant proceedings for genocide against named individuals. The Gambia told the court that he was among the generals the mission said should be investigated and prosecuted. paras 77, 523, 527, 545

    After Myanmar’s report, due within two years, the court will hear both sides and decide how the compensation phase proceeds. The judgment does not say what will follow if Myanmar does not comply. para 625
